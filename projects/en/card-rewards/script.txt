Inside the credit card industry, there is a secret, insulting nickname for people who pay their bills on time every single month.

Banks don’t call them loyal customers. They don’t call them responsible.

They call them deadbeats.

Think about how strange that is. You sign up for a sleek metal card, and suddenly a billion-dollar bank starts showering you with gifts. Five percent cashback. Free airport lounge access. Complimentary hotel nights. Round-trip flights paid entirely in points.

It feels like you’ve found a glitch in the financial system—like you’re finally beating the banks at their own game.

Except you’re not.

Credit card companies aren’t losing money on your free rewards. In fact, those rewards are the exact bait that helps them pull in over a hundred and thirty billion dollars a year in interest and fees alone.

Because in finance, nothing is ever truly free. Someone is always picking up the check. And when you look behind the curtain, you’ll realize your "free" rewards are being paid for by two very specific groups of people—and if you aren’t careful, you’re about to become one of them.

The first group paying for your points has no idea they’re doing it. They’re the people paying with cash and debit cards.

Every single time you tap your credit card at a coffee shop, a grocery store, or an online checkout, something invisible happens in the background. The store doesn’t get to keep a hundred percent of your money. The bank and the card network—like Visa or Mastercard—immediately slice off a cut called an interchange fee. Usually, it’s somewhere between two and three percent of the entire bill.

Now, merchants aren’t charities either. They aren’t going to quietly lose three percent of their revenue on every sale. So what do they do? They bake that fee straight into the price of every single item on the shelf.

That means whether you pay with a fancy rewards card or a plain twenty-dollar bill, you’re already paying a two to three percent markup. When you use a rewards card, the bank simply hands a tiny piece of that markup back to you and calls it "cashback." Meanwhile, the person in line behind you paying with cash pays the exact same inflated price—and gets nothing back. In effect, cash buyers are quietly subsidizing the airport lounges and free flights of credit card users.

That’s the first hidden engine. But interchange fees alone aren’t enough to fund massive sign-up bonuses and luxury perks.

For the real money, banks rely on the second group. The exact opposite of a deadbeat.

Inside the industry, they’re called revolvers.

A revolver is anyone who doesn’t pay their full balance at the end of the month—anyone who lets debt "revolve" into the next billing cycle. And the moment you leave even a fraction of your balance unpaid, the trap springs shut.

Credit card interest rates routinely sit between twenty-two and thirty-six percent per year. In many cases, even higher.

Let’s look at how brutal that math actually is. Imagine you put two thousand dollars on a card at twenty-four percent interest, and you only pay the minimum amount due each month. You wouldn’t just pay off two thousand dollars. You’d be stuck paying that bill for years, and you’d hand the bank well over a thousand dollars in pure interest.

That is what pays for the free flights. Banks will happily lose fifty dollars on a hundred "deadbeats" if it means hooking just a handful of revolvers who will pay them thousands in high-interest debt over the next decade. One bad month—one unexpected medical bill, one car repair, one forgotten payment—is all it takes to flip you from a winner into a profit center.

Now, you might be sitting there thinking, "That’s not me. I’m disciplined. I pay my balance in full every single month. I’m winning."

Maybe. But there’s a third layer to this game that catches even the smartest, most disciplined people off guard.

Years ago, researchers at MIT ran a famous experiment comparing how people spend with cash versus credit cards. They found that simply paying with a credit card made people willing to spend significantly more—on everyday purchases, studies consistently show people spend twelve to eighteen percent more with plastic than with cash.

Why? Because credit cards erase the pain of paying. Handing over physical cash feels like a loss right now. Swiping a piece of plastic feels like a promise you’ll deal with thirty days from now.

And banks supercharge this effect with gamification. "Spend four thousand dollars in ninety days to unlock sixty thousand bonus points." Suddenly, you’re upgrading your dinner, buying gadgets you didn’t plan on buying, and stretching your budget—all to chase a reward.

Think about the math on that. If a bank convinces you to spend an extra five hundred dollars you wouldn’t have spent, just so you can earn fifty dollars in cashback, you didn’t make fifty dollars. You lost four hundred and fifty.

And even when you do earn those points, banks profit from something called breakage—billions of dollars in rewards that sit unredeemed, expire, or quietly lose value every time an airline or bank devalues its points overnight.

So does that mean you should cut up your credit cards and go back to carrying envelopes of cash?

Not at all. The wealthiest people on the planet use credit cards for almost everything—for fraud protection, for building credit history, and yes, for the rewards.

The difference is they never play by the bank’s rules. They follow three simple rules of their own.

First: treat your credit card like a debit card with a delay. Never swipe for a single dollar that isn’t already sitting in your checking account today.

Second: turn on autopay for the full statement balance—never the minimum due. One single month of interest can wipe out an entire year of cashback.

And third: never chase a reward. Points and cashback should only ever be a side effect of money you were already going to spend—never the reason you spend it.

The next time a bank offers you a shiny metal card and a mountain of free points, remember what’s really happening. They aren’t being generous. They’re placing a calculated bet that sooner or later, you’ll overspend or slip up.

Your job is simple: take the perks, protect your cash, and be the exact kind of deadbeat the banks can’t stand.
