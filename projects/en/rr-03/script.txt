In 2013, Mark Zuckerberg cut his salary to one dollar a year. Steve Jobs did the exact same thing at Apple. So did Google's founders, Larry Page and Sergey Brin. When headlines announced this, the public applauded. It looked like the ultimate act of humility—CEOs working for free because they loved their companies. But in the world of ultra-high-net-worth finance, nothing is ever done for charity. Taking a one-dollar salary isn't a sacrifice. It is one of the smartest, most aggressive financial moves in the tax code. And once you understand the math behind it, you'll realize why the rich don't actually want a paycheck.

To understand the one-dollar salary trick, you first have to understand how the tax system classifies money. The government doesn't treat all dollars equally. It splits them into two main buckets: money you work for, and money your assets make for you. If you earn a regular salary—what the IRS calls ordinary earned income—you are placed in the highest tax bracket. In the United States, federal income tax alone climbs up to 37 percent. Add state taxes and payroll taxes, and a high earner in California or New York can lose nearly half of their paycheck before they even see it. Now imagine Mark Zuckerberg decided to pay himself a 100 million dollar salary. Almost 50 million of that would vanish instantly to the government. So why would a billionaire ever take cash through the most heavily taxed door in the entire financial system? They wouldn't. They take one dollar through the front door—and billions through the back.

When a CEO takes a one-dollar salary, they aren't working for free. They are taking their compensation in company stock—shares, stock options, and equity. And here is where the first major rule of the wealthy kicks in. If a billionaire eventually sells those shares after holding them for more than a year, the profit isn't taxed as a salary. It's taxed as a Long-Term Capital Gain. At the federal level, the maximum long-term capital gains tax rate is 20 percent—plus a 3.8 percent investment surtax, bringing it to 23.8 percent. Compare those two numbers: up to 37 percent federal tax on a paycheck, versus 20 percent on long-term stock gains. Just by changing the label of how they get paid, a billionaire immediately cuts their federal tax bill nearly in half. Even better? There are no Social Security or Medicare payroll taxes on capital gains. For an employee, the tax system punishes labor. For an owner, it rewards equity. But even 20 percent is too high for the ultra-wealthy. Because there is a second layer to this trick—one that drops the tax rate all the way to zero.

Here is the secret most people miss: you only pay capital gains tax when you sell. If a billionaire's stock grows by 500 million dollars this year, and they don't sell a single share, their taxable income from that gain is zero. In finance, this is called an unrealized gain. On paper, they are half a billion dollars richer. To the tax office, nothing happened. Now, you might be thinking: "That's great on paper, but you can't buy a yacht or a mansion with company shares. Eventually, they have to sell to get cash, right?" Actually, no. They don't. Instead of selling their stock and triggering a massive tax bill, billionaires walk into a private bank and say: "I own one billion dollars worth of stock. Lend me 50 million dollars in cash, and I'll use my shares as collateral." This is called a Securities-Backed Line of Credit, or SBLOC. Because the loan is backed by billions in assets, banks offer ultra-low interest rates. And here is the golden rule of the tax code: debt is not income. You do not pay income tax on borrowed money. So the billionaire gets 50 million dollars in cash to fund their lifestyle, pays zero percent in income tax, and keeps full ownership of their stock—which continues to grow faster than the interest on the loan.

Beyond taxes, the one-dollar salary does two more things that quietly make billionaires even richer. First, it's brilliant public relations. It sends a powerful signal to Wall Street and everyday investors: "I am so confident in this company that I only make money if the stock goes up." Investors love that alignment. They buy more shares. The stock price rises. And because the CEO owns millions of those shares, their net worth explodes by far more than any salary could ever pay them. Second, it keeps them in control. Selling shares means giving up voting power. Borrowing against them means keeping their throne—while still spending like a king.

The one-dollar salary was never about humility. It was a masterclass in understanding the rules of the game. The average person is taught to chase a bigger paycheck—only to hand a massive slice of it to taxes every single month. The wealthy know that a paycheck is the most expensive way to get rich. Real wealth isn't built on what you earn. It's built on what you own.
